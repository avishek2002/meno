# Cognitive offloading, the Google effect, and automation bias

## Cognitive offloading

[Risko and Gilbert](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27542527/), in Trends in
Cognitive Sciences, define cognitive offloading as "the use of physical action to
alter the information processing requirements of a task so as to reduce cognitive
demand" - tilting your head to read rotated text, setting a phone reminder instead of
remembering, or, in the present context, letting an agent draft the thing you were
going to think through. Their metacognitive framework asks two questions: what
triggers people to offload, and what are the cognitive consequences when they do.
Offloading is often rational - it extends what a person can do - but the framework's
point is that the decision to offload and its cognitive consequences need separate
accounting: what reduces demand in the moment can still change what the person
retains, which is exactly what the Google-effect studies below measure.

## The Google effect

[Sparrow, Liu, and Wegner](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21764755/) showed, across
four studies published in Science, that when people expect future access to
information, they remember the information itself less well and remember where to
find it better. Hard questions primed participants to think of computers; the
internet functions as a transactive memory store - an external partner that holds
knowledge collectively so individuals do not have to. The finding predates modern
assistants, but the mechanism transfers: material you expect the agent to reproduce
on demand is material your own memory quietly declines to keep.

## Automation bias

[Goddard, Roudsari, and Wyatt](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3240751/),
in a systematic review in the Journal of the American Medical Informatics
Association, describe automation bias as users over-accepting computer output "as a
heuristic replacement of vigilant information seeking and processing." It shows up as
commission errors (following incorrect advice) and omission errors (failing to act
because the system did not prompt). The review found the effect robust across
research fields; in the clinical studies reviewed, wrong advice from a decision
support system measurably flipped decisions that had been correct before the advice,
with negative consultations in the range of 6 to 11 percent across prospective
studies. Mediating factors included user experience, confidence, and trust; task complexity,
workload, and time constraints; and the system's design and reliability.

## Why these three belong together

Offloading explains why the human stops doing the work; the Google effect shows the
memory cost of anticipated access; automation bias shows the vigilance cost at the
moment of use. Together they describe how the person in an agent-assisted workflow
becomes the weakest check on the agent's output precisely because the workflow is
comfortable.
