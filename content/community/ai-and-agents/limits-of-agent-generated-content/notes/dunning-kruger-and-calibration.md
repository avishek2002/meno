# The Dunning-Kruger effect and calibration

## The original finding and its dual burden

[Kruger and Dunning](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10626367/), in the Journal of
Personality and Social Psychology, showed that participants scoring in the bottom
quartile on tests of humor, grammar, and logic dramatically overestimated themselves:
scoring on average at the 12th percentile, they estimated themselves at the 62nd. The
explanation is a dual burden - the skills that produce competent performance are
largely the same skills needed to evaluate performance, so the unskilled make
mistakes and lack the means to notice them. Crucially, when participants' actual
skill was improved through training, their self-assessments improved too: the deficit
is metacognitive, and it moves with the skill itself.

## The finding is not an artifact of incentives or noise

[Ehrlinger and colleagues](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2702783/), in
Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes, tested the standard objections.
Correcting for measurement unreliability did not remove the bottom performers'
overestimates on real-world tasks such as college exams and debate tournaments.
Offering money (up to 100 United States dollars) or social accountability for
accurate self-assessment did not improve accuracy either, and sometimes increased
overconfidence. The metacognitive explanation survived its critics' tests: people are
not misreporting out of laziness or self-presentation; they genuinely cannot see the
gap.

## Overconfidence is three findings, not one

[Moore and Healy](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18426301/), in Psychological
Review, showed that "overconfidence" mixes three distinct effects: overestimation
(thinking you did better than you did), overplacement (thinking you rank higher than
others than you do), and overprecision (holding your beliefs with too-narrow error
bars). These pattern differently: hard tasks produce overestimation alongside
underplacement, easy tasks the reverse, while overprecision persists most robustly of
the three. Anyone auditing their own calibration needs to know which of the three
they are measuring, because a fix for one can leave the others untouched.

## What calibration practice follows from this

The research implies self-assessment cannot be trusted as a felt sense; it has to be
measured. That means recording explicit predictions with confidence levels before
checking answers, comparing stated confidence to observed accuracy over many
predictions, and treating a persistent gap as the finding rather than an exception.
Because the deficit shrinks as skill grows, checked practice with feedback is both
the calibration instrument and part of the cure.
